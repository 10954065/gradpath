## Introduction

Students often find research philosophy the most abstract part of a research methods course. Words such as *ontology*, *epistemology* and *axiology* sound remote from the practical task of studying, for instance, how Ghanaian hospitals adopt electronic medical records. Yet these ideas determine what you believe you are studying, what counts as acceptable evidence, and how you handle your own values. A study whose philosophy contradicts its methods is incoherent, and examiners notice incoherence quickly.

This lesson unpacks the three branches of philosophy that underpin research paradigms and, most importantly, shows how to link philosophical commitments to concrete design decisions. By the end you should be able to write a short, coherent philosophical justification that flows logically into your choice of approach, strategy, sampling and analysis, rather than a detached paragraph of definitions that bears no relation to the rest of your proposal.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain ontology, epistemology and axiology using examples from information technology and business research.
- Distinguish objectivist and constructionist ontologies and positivist and interpretivist epistemologies.
- Analyse how axiological assumptions influence a researcher's role and the handling of values and bias.
- Apply the "research onion" logic to connect philosophical assumptions to approach, strategy, time horizon and techniques.
- Evaluate the internal coherence of a proposed research design.

## Prerequisite Knowledge

- Understanding of positivism, interpretivism and pragmatism (previous lesson).
- The distinction between methodology and methods.

## Key Terminology

- **Ontology**: The branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of reality and what exists; in research, assumptions about the nature of the phenomena being studied.
- **Objectivism**: An ontological position holding that social entities exist independently of the people who are part of them.
- **Constructionism**: An ontological position holding that social phenomena are created and continually revised through social interaction and shared meanings. Also called constructivism in some texts.
- **Epistemology**: The branch of philosophy concerned with knowledge: what counts as valid knowledge and how it can be obtained.
- **Axiology**: The branch of philosophy concerned with values, including the role of the researcher's values and ethics in research.
- **Reflexivity**: The researcher's ongoing, critical reflection on how their background, assumptions and presence influence the research.
- **Methodological coherence**: The logical consistency between philosophical assumptions, research questions, design, methods and claims.
- **Abduction**: Reasoning that moves between data and theory to identify the most plausible explanation for surprising observations.

## Detailed Explanation

### Ontology: what is the nature of the thing being studied?

Ontology asks what reality is like. In business and IT research the question becomes concrete: is an "organisational culture of cybersecurity" an objective structure that exists whether or not employees recognise it, or is it a set of meanings that employees continuously construct and negotiate?

An **objectivist** ontology treats social phenomena such as organisations, markets or technology systems as having an existence external to social actors, rather like physical objects. An organisation's formal hierarchy, the number of transactions processed by a payment platform or the configuration of a firewall can reasonably be treated this way. A **constructionist** ontology argues that social phenomena are produced through interaction and interpretation. "Trust in mobile money" is not a fixed object; it is formed and reformed through experiences, conversations, rumours of fraud and relationships with agents.

Ontology is rarely all-or-nothing. Critical realism, for instance, combines a belief that reality exists independently of us with an acceptance that our understanding of it is socially conditioned. What matters is that you recognise which assumption your study makes about its core phenomenon.

### Epistemology: what counts as acceptable knowledge?

Epistemology concerns how we can know things and what counts as warranted knowledge. A **positivist** epistemology holds that acceptable knowledge comes from observable, measurable phenomena and that the researcher should remain independent of what is studied. Credible knowledge, in this view, consists of generalisable relationships established through careful measurement and testing.

An **interpretivist** epistemology holds that knowledge of the social world requires understanding the subjective meanings people attach to their actions. Here, credible knowledge includes participants' accounts, narratives and interpretations, analysed carefully by a researcher who acknowledges their own interpretive role.

Crotty (1998) offers a helpful way to see the relationships: epistemology informs a theoretical perspective, which informs methodology, which in turn informs methods. The direction matters. You should not choose interviews first and then hunt for an epistemology to justify them.

### Axiology: what role do values play?

Axiology addresses the role of values in research. Positivist research aspires to value-neutrality, attempting to minimise the researcher's influence through standardised instruments and procedures. Interpretivist research accepts that research is value-laden and asks the researcher to be **reflexive**: to state how their background and assumptions may shape interpretation. Pragmatist researchers acknowledge that values influence which problems are chosen and how results are used.

Axiology also connects directly to ethics. Your values influence whom you choose to study, whose voice is heard, and how you protect participants. A researcher studying technology adoption in Ghanaian healthcare who is also an employee of the hospital must consider how that dual role affects what staff are willing to say and how findings are interpreted.

### Linking philosophy to design

The value of philosophy lies in its consequences for design. Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill's "research onion" is a widely used model that visualises these links as layers: philosophy, approach to theory development, methodological choice, strategy, time horizon, and finally techniques and procedures at the core. The outer layers constrain and justify the inner ones. A coherent design is one where each layer follows from the previous one.

## Theories, Frameworks and Models

### The research onion (adapted)

The table below translates the onion's layers into the decisions an MSc student must make, with typical choices for two contrasting positions.

| Layer | Decision | Objectivist and positivist study | Constructionist and interpretivist study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Philosophy | Assumptions about reality and knowledge | Reality is external and measurable | Reality is socially constructed |
| Approach to theory | Deductive, inductive or abductive | Deductive: test hypotheses from theory | Inductive: build understanding from data |
| Methodological choice | Mono, multi or mixed method | Mono-method quantitative | Mono-method qualitative |
| Strategy | Survey, experiment, case study and so on | Cross-sectional survey | Case study or ethnography |
| Time horizon | Cross-sectional or longitudinal | Usually cross-sectional | Cross-sectional or longitudinal |
| Techniques | Data collection and analysis | Questionnaire, statistical analysis | Interviews, thematic analysis |

### Deduction, induction and abduction

- **Deduction** starts with theory, derives hypotheses and tests them. It sits naturally with positivism.
- **Induction** starts with data and builds explanations or theory. It sits naturally with interpretivism.
- **Abduction** moves back and forth, using surprising observations to refine theory. It is common in pragmatist and critical realist research.

## Worked Example

**Illustrative scenario.** Kofi proposes to study cybersecurity awareness among undergraduate students at a Ghanaian university. His draft says: "This study adopts an interpretivist philosophy. A questionnaire with 30 Likert-scale items will be administered to 400 students and the data analysed using regression to test hypotheses."

**Step 1: Identify the inconsistency.** An interpretivist epistemology emphasises subjective meanings and in-depth understanding, but the design is deductive, highly structured and statistical. The philosophy and methods contradict one another.

**Step 2: Clarify the question.** Kofi's real interest is: *To what extent do security training, prior victimisation and self-efficacy predict students' security behaviour?* This is a question about measurable relationships across a population.

**Step 3: Realign the philosophy.** An objectivist ontology (behaviour and its predictors can be treated as measurable) and a positivist or post-positivist epistemology fit. Axiologically, he aims to minimise bias through validated scales and anonymous responses.

**Step 4: Check downstream coherence.** Deductive approach, survey strategy, cross-sectional time horizon, questionnaire and regression analysis now align.

**Alternative.** If Kofi were instead interested in *how students understand online risk and why they ignore security advice*, he could retain interpretivism but should change to semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis.

The expected learning is that philosophy is not decorative; it must match the question and the design.

## Ghanaian Business Application

Philosophical choices become very practical in Ghanaian technology research. Consider the introduction of electronic health records in public hospitals.

- A **health administrator** might want to know whether the system reduced patient waiting times. This invites an objectivist ontology (waiting time is measurable), a positivist epistemology and perhaps a before-and-after comparison using hospital records.
- A **researcher interested in nurses' experiences** might ask how the system changed professional identities and workloads. Here, a constructionist ontology and interpretivist epistemology fit, using interviews and observation on wards.
- A **ministry project team** seeking practical recommendations for national rollout might adopt pragmatism, combining performance data with staff interviews.

Axiology is especially important in Ghanaian organisational settings, where hierarchy and respect for seniority may influence how openly junior staff discuss problems with a researcher perceived as aligned with management. A reflexive researcher acknowledges this, perhaps by interviewing staff away from supervisors and assuring confidentiality. Research involving patient data must also respect the Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843), reflecting that values and legal obligations shape design choices.

## International Case Study

### Orlikowski and Baroudi on IS research assumptions

In 1991, Wanda Orlikowski and Jack Baroudi published "Studying Information Technology in Organizations: Research Approaches and Assumptions" in *Information Systems Research*. They examined published information systems research and classified it according to its underlying philosophical assumptions, distinguishing positivist, interpretive and critical perspectives. They found that positivist assumptions dominated the field and argued that this narrowed the kinds of questions researchers asked about technology in organisations.

Their article has been widely cited as part of the case for philosophical pluralism in information systems research. It shows that philosophical assumptions are often implicit, and that making them explicit helps researchers see which questions they are, and are not, able to answer. For an MSc student in IT for Business, the paper is a reminder that your philosophical stance should be a deliberate choice, made visible in your methodology chapter, rather than an unexamined default.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** Ontology and epistemology are the same thing. **Correction:** Ontology concerns what reality is; epistemology concerns how we can know it and what counts as knowledge.
- **Misconception:** Philosophy is a box to tick at the start of the methodology chapter. **Correction:** It should explain and justify every later design choice.
- **Misconception:** Axiology only matters in qualitative research. **Correction:** Values shape topic choice, measurement and interpretation in all research, including quantitative studies.
- **Misconception:** A researcher can be simultaneously objectivist and constructionist about the same phenomenon without explanation. **Correction:** Combining positions requires a reasoned framework such as critical realism or pragmatism.

## Common Examination Mistakes

- Giving dictionary definitions of ontology and epistemology without linking them to research design.
- Writing a philosophy section that contradicts the stated methods.
- Omitting axiology or treating it as identical to ethics approval.
- Spelling or using the terms inconsistently, for example "epistemological approach" when describing a data collection technique.
- Failing to explain how the chosen philosophy shapes the claims the study can make.

## Practical Exercises

1. **Coherence audit.** Take the following design: "Pragmatist philosophy; inductive approach; experiment; questionnaire; thematic analysis." Identify every inconsistency and propose a coherent alternative. *Expected output:* a list of at least three mismatches and a revised six-layer design table.
2. **Philosophy paragraph.** For a study of e-commerce adoption among small businesses in Tamale, write a 200-word philosophical justification covering ontology, epistemology and axiology. *Expected output:* one paragraph that connects each assumption to a specific design decision.
3. **Reflexivity statement.** Write a 100-word reflexive statement for a study in which you interview colleagues about a new IT system at your workplace. *Expected output:* a statement identifying your position, potential influence on participants and the steps you will take to manage it.

## Key Takeaways

- Ontology concerns the nature of reality; epistemology concerns what counts as knowledge; axiology concerns the role of values.
- Objectivism and constructionism are contrasting ontologies; positivism and interpretivism are contrasting epistemologies.
- Philosophy should flow logically into approach, strategy, time horizon and techniques.
- Coherence between layers is a key indicator of methodological quality.
- Reflexivity helps researchers manage the influence of their values and position.

## Revision Summary

Ontology asks what reality is (objectivism versus constructionism), epistemology asks what counts as valid knowledge (positivism versus interpretivism) and axiology asks how values influence research. These assumptions must lead coherently to the approach to theory, methodological choice, strategy, time horizon and techniques, as visualised in the research onion.

- Objectivism: social phenomena exist independently of actors; constructionism: they are created through interaction.
- Positivist epistemology values measurable, generalisable evidence; interpretivist epistemology values meanings.
- Axiology requires reflexivity and ethical awareness.
- Deduction, induction and abduction connect philosophy to theory development.
- Incoherence between philosophy and methods is a serious weakness.
